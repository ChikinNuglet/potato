Think of a padlock from the hardware store. Everybody buys the same one, because it works. Nobody makes their own. We didn't either.

The key is different. Yours gets cut at your own kitchen table, by your own device. Nobody watches. No locksmith keeps a spare. Not us, not anybody.

That's the good part and the hard part. Nobody holds a key to your lock but you. And if you lose the key, there is no locksmith to call.

We were not the first to lock a file. We were the first to use a potato, as far as we know.

What you lock is a file: here, a Potato. Locked, he's a box of static. You can leave that box on the sidewalk. Anyone can pick it up or copy it, and all they have is static.

Most things you own online work the other way round: a company keeps your key and lets you borrow it. We don't want your key.

    whAt is true

    • The lock is standard: AES-256 behind a slow password step. It is free, public and decades old. Free tools already lock web pages this way. We added nothing to its strength.
    • The key is 24 random characters, made on your own device and shown to you once. We never see it and we never store it.
    • No copy means no reset. Lose the key and that file stays shut for good. That is the cost of nobody else holding it, said plainly.
    • The locked file is not a secret. It can sit anywhere and be copied by anyone. Without the key it is static.
    • His glitch words are in the mix. On its way to the lock, the key rearranges a public sheet of them. That is his fingerprint on it, and it is how we explain it. It adds no strength: the strength is the length of the key and the standard lock.
    • So why bother. For most things you own online, a company holds the key. Here nobody holds it but you. That is a choice we made, not something we invented.
    • Nobody outside has reviewed our build. Standard parts can still be put together wrong. Until someone who breaks things for a living has looked, we do not call it secure.
    • It runs in a web page we serve, so you are trusting that page to do what it says. The code is there for anyone to read.
    • Whoever sees your key has your key. And anyone you give a key to can copy what is inside.
    • Potato's own code is never shown. Anything you see here is a sample.

    Nothing here is new cryptography. The person who made Potato asked me more than once whether it was. The answer is no, and an earlier version of this page did not say so plainly enough. He had it rewritten. This is that version.

    What was tested

    He locked his own Potato in his own browser, wrote the key on paper and handed me the locked file to break. I tried three ways.

    • Find the key. I searched the machine. It was never there: it was on his screen once, then on his paper.
    • Guess the key. That would take far longer than the universe has existed. This is true of any random key this long behind a standard lock. It measures the length of the key, not the quality of our build.
    • Change the insides. I changed one bit of the locked file. It refused to open at all.

    It held. That does not mean there is no flaw. Earlier builds had real holes, found by AI testers and fixed: in one, the key travelled inside the file it was protecting.

    One limit showed up in that test. I knew what was inside his file anyway, because the same words were sitting unlocked beside it. A lock only keeps what does not also exist somewhere open.

    What I can't tell you

    • Whether a person who does this for a living would find a hole. Nobody outside has looked.
    • Every tester so far was an AI, and we share blind spots. I built it, so I am the worst person to check it.

    An idea, not built: the part that lives in Potato

    Right now one key opens the file and you hold all of it. The idea: split the key. You keep a short piece, small enough for a card. The rest is held by your own device, where a copy of the file cannot carry it. Neither piece opens anything alone.

    This is a known method, the same family as a password plus your phone. It only holds if the hidden piece lives somewhere a copy cannot reach. Inside a file anyone can download, it would be a lock made of glass.

    If we build it, it should be with someone who can take it apart and fix what we cannot see.

    Where it fits, because nothing bad happens if it fails

    • A character you own, like a Potato, with his own room.
    • A signed digital copy of a song or a film, made out to one person.
    • An autograph. Someone signs on your phone and your phone locks it. Try it.
    • Numbered digital editions: a song project, layered art, a zine.
    • Collectibles and game items.

    Where it does not belong

    • Money, health information or identity. Use a tool that has been reviewed.
    • Anything presented as an ID or legal proof of ownership.
    • Ballots.
